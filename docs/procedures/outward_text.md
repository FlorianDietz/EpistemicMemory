# Outward Text: avoiding LLM-isms

Read before finalizing ANY text that goes to people outside the project: emails, blog or forum posts, grant applications, papers, presentations, public README text. Text that reads as detectably LLM-written triggers immediate aversion in many readers (some forums moderate for it explicitly) and can discredit good content even when a human approved every line. Scope: outward text only. Internal project files (LOGs, SUMMARYs) are exempt.

**Principles (these matter more than any word list):**
- **Density, not presence.** One em-dash or one "crucial" is fine; *clusters* of tells are what registers. Remove clusters; don't mangle correct writing to dodge individual words.
- **The skeleton is the loudest tell.** Word-level cleanup does not rescue an AI-shaped structure: uniform section scaffolding, bold-label patterns, identically-shaped objection/response or list entries, and same-cadence paragraphs register as AI even with clean vocabulary. Reflow structure into prose with uneven rhythm; keep only lists that are genuine enumerations.
- **Add human markers, don't just delete AI markers:** varied sentence length and rhythm; concrete specifics (real numbers, names, dates, own experience); a clear position instead of a balanced survey; mild informality (contractions, an occasional fragment). Strongest marker: at least one passage only the author could have written, such as personal experience with something at stake, including where something failed. Source such material from the project files or ask the user for it; NEVER invent it.
- **Read it aloud once.** Wherever the rhythm is metronomic, or a sentence could appear unchanged in anyone's text about anything, rewrite it.

**Checklist of tells** (compiled 2026-07, last refreshed 2026-10-10). Tell profiles are model-dependent and go stale within months; re-verify roughly monthly if you publish regularly, and always before a high-stakes submission. **Refresh rule: read full primary sources.** Every claim that enters this file is checked against the full primary article, paper, or policy page, not search snippets, abstracts, secondary coverage, or a sub-agent's summary of them. Mark anything that could only be reached second-hand as such.

**Know which model drafted the text.** Tell profiles now differ per model *and version*: a new version relocates its tells rather than shedding them (Graphite's web-article corpus, where a "tell" is a phrase a model uses at ≥2× the rate of pre-ChatGPT human articles on the same ~10,000 topics: Opus 5 → Opus 5.5 went from 2,666 to 2,548 such phrases, only 4% fewer, while its em-dash rate fell 99%; [study](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-tells), [Opus 5.5 update](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-tells-opus-5-5-update)). Sweep with the drafting model's current profile first (below: "Opus 5.5", "GPT-6 Astra", "Gemini 3.1 Pro"), then the general list. If the drafting model is newer than everything listed here, the list is out of date: refresh it first.

1. *Lexical:* delve, tapestry, testament, crucial/pivotal/vital, landscape/realm, underscore/highlight/showcase, leverage, robust, seamless, meticulous, intricate, multifaceted, comprehensive, foster, bolster, embark, vibrant, profound, groundbreaking, transformative, "deep dive", "it's worth noting", "plays a crucial role", "navigate the complexities", "in today's fast-paced world". Era note (2026-08): the delve/tapestry/meticulous class is a 2023–24 tell largely trained out of current models. Still flag it (it marks older-model text or humans imitating it), but current Claude-5 output tells are these instead: "carry the argument", "worth stating plainly", ", and the trap", "The X matters more", "move" as a noun, "full stop" as an emphasis tag, "You're absolutely right." as an opener, "the honest answer", "quiet(ly)" as a contentless intensifier ("quietly building"). Claude up to Opus 5 / Fable 5: "genuine(ly)" and "honest(ly)" (measured at ~10× other models), performative enthusiasm ("exciting", "powerful"), unsolicited caveats, "load-bearing". Opus 5.5 cut several of these (80-post blog study, LLM-judge counts: verdict intensifiers like "the honest answer" ~−65%, stock metaphors like "load-bearing" ~−70%; "is genuinely" 26× rarer than in Opus 5), but "honestly/frankly" fell only ~35% (LMArena chat data), so treat them as weak signals for Opus 5.5. Adverb-fronted appraisal clusters ("counterintuitively", "undeniably", "trivially", "neatly", "precisely the..."). **Opus 5.5** (Graphite web-article corpus 2026-10; ratios vs pre-ChatGPT human articles unless noted): importance-flagging is the leading theme: "this matters" (~116× human rate), "why X matters", "matters most", "just as important" (salience flags are also the most common Claudism in an independent blog-post study); evaluative adjectives "dependable", "steady", "thoughtful", "meaningful" (dependable/steady are shared with GPT-6 Astra, so they don't identify the model); transitions "looking ahead", "adds another layer", "what comes next", "in practice"; helpfulness phrasing ("is especially helpful", "can help you", "makes it easier"; vs Opus 5, not vs humans); superlatives ("perhaps the most", "one of the best"; vs GPT-6 Astra); "perhaps"/"arguably" ~2× Opus 5's rate (LMArena chat data), but Opus 5 was the Claude low and Opus 4.6–4.7 matched 5.5, so this is a weak signal. **GPT-6 Astra**: "matters because", "another dimension", "together these", "without requiring", "practical", "dependable", "may provide" (vs Claude); weaker evidence (Wikipedia list citing one product review): "prioritize", "clearer", sentences held in an ~8–20-word band. **Gemini 3.1 Pro**: "ultimately this", "furthermore the", "absolutely essential", "immense", "incredibly", near-absence of contractions. Phrase-level ratios run huge for phrases humans almost never use, so one "this matters" is not a tell; the density principle still governs.
2. *Punctuation/formatting:* dash overuse (em-dash or hyphen; readers don't distinguish them). A handful per essay is fine, but prefer a dash-free phrasing (comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence) whenever it reads equally well. Era-scoped: dashes mark Claude chat/blog text up to Opus 5 and Fable 5.1 (and older GPT text); Opus 5.5 nearly eliminated em-dashes (−95 to −99% vs Opus 5) and cut semicolons ~−73%, and GPT models were trained away from them earlier, so a dash-free draft is no longer evidence of human authorship. Models otherwise UNDER-punctuate (Economist study, 2026-07, of ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini/Grok: fewer commas, semicolons and parentheses than humans, long sentences with "and" as the most overused word, paragraphs rarely broken by a short sentence), so unbroken smoothness is itself a tell; since then Opus 5.5 has shortened its sentences (~12 → ~10 words). Humans use far more exclamation marks and parenthetical asides than the current models measured. Claude up to Opus 5: colon/semicolon overuse replacing ordinary connectives, incl. the colon-reveal (setup clause, colon, neat payload). Opus 5.5 went the other way on formatting: ~2.5× the bold lead-in bullets and ~+35% three-item lists vs Opus 5. Also: **bold-term:** colon lists, bullets/headings where prose is expected, rule-of-three triplets everywhere, emoji bullets, Title Case Headings, uniform sentence lengths, markdown leakage (stray `**`, `#`).
3. *Structure/rhetoric:* "it's not just X, it's Y" contrast frames, incl. negation-then-inversion openers ("was not X. It was Y."). As an essay's opening move this is the single most recognized AI signature. Newer models reword rather than drop the frame, so sweep for its variants too: "is more than a X, it…", "rather than simply", "not only about" (Opus 5.5), "less like a X and more like" (Opus 5), "the X is not simply", "rather than relying" (GPT-6 Astra), "is not just a X, it is" (Gemini 3.1 Pro). Chiasmus/balanced aphorisms with matched clause lengths ("who questions nothing is defenseless; who questions everything learns nothing"): humans produce at most ~one per essay. Aphoristic paragraph-closers on every paragraph ("zinger cadence": each paragraph 3–5 sentences landing on a short declarative beat); roadmap sentences ("I'll argue below that…"); restating conclusions ("In summary", "Overall"); dangling "-ing" significance clauses ("…highlighting its significance"); sycophantic openers ("Great question!"); hedging both-sides-ism with no stance; identical paragraph shapes; "from X to Y" sweeps; "studies show"/"experts agree" with nothing named. (Contrast frames, triplets, uniform cadence, and aphoristic closers were re-verified with corpus data in 2026-08; ChatGPT and Claude are the heaviest users. Do not prune these as stale.) Mostly observed in Claude-5 models: copula avoidance ("serves as"/"stands as"/"functions as"/"represents" where "is" would do); confidence by litotes ("it is not difficult to see that…", asserting by negating the opposite); restatement gloss ("in other words", "put differently"); self-ranking one's own claims ("most important", "the cleanest organizing idea"); staged self-correction ("It would be wrong, though, to call this…"); the reframe ("Better posed: …"); suspense hooks and stakes-raising ("has a name", "shapes everything that follows"); vague connection phrasing ("in connection with", "associated with"); empty commentary (sentences that sound analytical but carry no evidence or specific). From Wikipedia editors' current signs (2026-10): reading-instruction disclaimers on sources ("should be treated as religious tradition rather than as an archaeological explanation"); over-attribution (name-dropping coverage and source types such as "featured in" or "independent coverage", and attributing the model's own analysis to a cited source); canned emphasis on media coverage. Also persisting in Opus 5.5 (blog-post study): signposts ("Here's the part that…", "The short answer is") and the gotcha frame ("The trap is", "The catch is").
4. *Tone:* relentless positivity, press-release puffery, no specific opinion, generic examples. Register (measured on Opus 5): prompt-contingent. Formal prompts produce the classic Latinate register ("utilize", "commence"), but DEFAULT output drifts the opposite way (short Germanic words, heavy "-ly" adverbs such as "entirely", "directly", "exactly", and contractions). Uniform register in either direction is the tell; human text mixes.
5. *Revision artifacts (a self-containment failure that doubles as an AI tell):* contrastive phrasing left over from edits ("Many parents *do* teach...", a "However" or "actually" answering nothing) reads as refuting a claim that only existed in an earlier draft, which the reader doesn't have. After any revision round, re-read the final text start-to-finish as a first-time reader. This is the outward-text case of `self_contained_check.md` (its step 4, history references, covers the same failure).
6. *Content (reads as "nobody vouched for this"):* citations attributed to an organization instead of named authors ("Some Lab 2025" where the paper has authors; reads like a model filling a gap); any citation not verified against the primary source before publishing (minimum: fetch the source, confirm title, authors, and that it makes the cited claim); terminology that is locally plausible but technically wrong (e.g. calling training samples "false positives" when they are negatives), so sweep technical terms for this class of error; **numbers over insight**: a figure quoted without its meaning stated alongside (baseline, comparison, or consequence) is filler that fakes rigor, and grant evaluators have named it as an LLM tell. Give every number its interpretation in the same sentence, or cut the number.

**False-positive caveat:** em-dashes and formal register are also human (academics especially), and detectors are unreliable in both directions. In an independent test (2026-08, Pangram 3.2 and GPTZero), humanizer paraphrasing defeated detection almost completely, lightly AI-polished human abstracts were flagged 38–80% of the time, and even recent human abstracts were flagged 9–15% depending on field; Pangram claims its v4 fixes both, measured only by Pangram. Detectors need ≥50 words and are weaker on short text. The goal is reception by experienced human readers, not fooling a detector.

**Known screening (as of 2026-10-10; venue policies change, so re-check the venue's current policy before submitting):**
- **LessWrong** (policy since 2026-03-14): "LLM output" (fully LLM-written, substantially LLM-edited human text, or human-edited LLM text; human text only *lightly* LLM-edited does not count) must sit in "LLM content blocks" whose header names the model; per moderators, block contents are excluded from automated detection. Enforcement: posts by approved users are auto-rejected above a detector threshold (apparently Pangram, per moderator comments), and the threshold is being lowered; first-time users face a near-zero-LLM bar. Escape hatch (moderator comment, not formal policy): before publishing, the author can ask the mods via Intercom to skip content blocks for heavier AI editing they fully stand behind. The superseded 2025 policy's rules (significant added value, full vouching, no stereotypical AI style, ≥1 minute of editing per 50 words) are no longer formal policy but remain a sensible bar; remind the user that this editing obligation is theirs, not the agent's. Votes: LessWrong's FAQ has a hard rule against mass voting ("mass downvoting, mass upvoting, or voting with sockpuppet accounts"), and the EA Forum states the broader norm outright: "Just don't ask for upvotes". So when the user announces a post to contacts, the ask is for thoughts, never for votes.
- **EA Forum** (since 2026-05): every post (not comments or quick takes) is auto-labeled from Pangram scores (no label / "Partly" / "Mostly" / "~Entirely AI Written"); no rejection for AI use, though low-quality LLM posts can be moved off the Frontpage, and the label is public. The author can preview the label by saving a draft; have the user do so before publishing.
- **Pangram**: for LessWrong- or EA-Forum-bound text, the final version should go through Pangram before submission. Ask the user to run it (pangram.com; do not create accounts yourself; free plan 20 checks/day after signup, paid from $20/month). Pangram 4 specifically targets AI-assisted and mixed-authorship text, i.e. the revision regime this procedure produces; in a vendor test it flagged 997 of 1,000 unedited Opus 5.5 outputs as fully AI. If flagged, do another structural pass (skeleton, cadence) before spending a re-check. Substack: readers can request a Pangram scan of a post, note or comment over 100 words (result shown only to them); authors can disable it per post.
- **arXiv**: relies on moderators spotting hard evidence rather than on detectors (per press coverage; no formal arXiv statement), and since 2026-05 issues one-year submission bans for unchecked AI generation, specifically hallucinated references and leftover chatbot meta-comments, after which submissions must first be accepted at a peer-reviewed venue. The citation gate below covers the first; a final read-through covers the second. Separately, CS review and position papers need proof of prior journal or conference peer review (workshops don't count).
- **Conferences**: ICLR 2027 requires a mandatory AI-use section in the paper (outside the page limit) plus form disclosure. Substantive uses (incl. translation) must be disclosed; drafting/editing help is only recommended for disclosure. LLM-produced falsehoods are a Code-of-Ethics violation that can lead to desk rejection. NeurIPS 2026 screened its position-paper track with Pangram, desk-rejected 178 of ~970 (18.4%, no appeal), and made 123 more prove human authorship.
- **Watermarking**: Anthropic watermarks Claude text (token-selection bias; no visible characters or metadata) for all models launched on or after 2026-08-02; its help page (2026-10-08) shows text watermarks live for Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, Fable 5/5.1, Mythos 5.1, Opus 5, Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.8, while Mythos 5 and older 4.x models are still pending. Detection needs Anthropic's key: a detection API is in private preview for EU-law-eligible organizations (e.g. regulators, media, fact-checkers, researchers, educators) and compliance-obligated enterprises, with wider access planned. OpenAI announced on 2026-10-05 that its text watermark is rolling out to EU ChatGPT/Codex users "over the coming weeks" (opt-in worldwide via the API; detector limited to approved researchers and expert organizations). Gemini text is SynthID-watermarked; Google's public SynthID Detector (opened 2026-10-07) checks only images, video and audio. Assume vendor attribution survives editing: per Anthropic, light editing probably won't remove the watermark and only a complete rewrite will (translations stay watermarked). So prose drafted by a watermarking model should be assumed attributable by the vendor and, increasingly, by third parties, even after this procedure's editing.

## Writing in the user's voice (roleplay instead of rules)

Status: experimental. Hypothesis: drafting *as the user*, roleplaying their voice from real samples, produces more human-reading text than drafting neutrally and removing tells afterwards. Evidence so far is thin: one comparison by this template's author on a single forum post, scored by Pangram. The rules-based draft scored 94% AI and the voice draft 88%, so the voice draft was better but still failed, and in both runs the detector flagged some of the author's own sentences as AI while passing some agent-written ones. A person's natural voice and what detectors accept can diverge, so a failed detector run does not by itself falsify the voice; human readers are the better judge.

### First use: collect writing samples

Each user's voice profile lives in `convos/{user}/voice_profile.md` (git-tracked, like the rest of the user's folder), using the template at the end of this file. If the current user has no profile yet and you are about to draft outward text for them, ask them first:

1. **Ask for samples.** Request 3–5 pieces they wrote without AI help, ideally including one in the register of the text you're about to draft (an argumentative post, an email, a grant text). Ask for file paths or links, or have them paste text into a file in the repo. Samples from the private folder (`convos/{user}/private/`) are private content under CLAUDE.md §Privacy: list them, and any verbatim examples taken from them, in a separate `convos/{user}/private/voice_profile.md`, never in the tracked profile, and quote them into outward text only if the user marks the passage shareable (`SHARE:` or an explicit instruction).
2. **Offer the guaranteed-human source in this repo.** The user's `C:` comments and bare top-of-file requests in LOGs are human-written by convention, so they are authorship-guaranteed samples for free. Find the longer, argumentative ones (`grep -rn --exclude-dir=private "C:" convos/{user}/`; bare requests are the untagged text at the top of a LOG or between `---` separators without an entry header) and list them with a grep phrase to locate each.
3. **Ask about AI assistance.** For every sample the user supplies, ask whether any part had LLM help. Record the answer in the profile. Anything with substantial LLM help goes in the excluded tier. Its skeleton is the model's even if the words were edited, and studying it would teach you the model's habits, not the user's.
4. **Draft the voice sketch.** Read the samples in full, then fill in the profile below: 5–8 bullets on how the user actually writes (how they open, sentence rhythm, punctuation habits, how they hedge, humor, recurring analogies or examples, what they never do), each with a short verbatim example from the samples. Show it to the user and ask them to correct it. Record the profile's creation in the current topic's LOG.
5. If the user declines to provide samples, skip this section and use the rules-based route (checklist sweep only). Create the profile file with just that note, so you don't ask every time.

### How to use a profile

Read 2–3 Tier-1 samples and at least one Tier-2 sample in full immediately before drafting, then write in first person as the user, matching their rhythm and habits. This replaces the *drafting* style only. The checklist sweep, fresh sub-agent read, citation gate, and detector check in the procedure below still run afterwards as a backstop. Don't over-polish: small imperfections that survive the user's own editing are part of the voice. Never fake typos.

**Reviewer-feedback adjudication:** when a blind reviewer flags a phrase in a voice draft as an LLM tell, grep the vetted samples before acting. Keep the phrase if it is attested in the user's own writing, cut it if not. Blind reviewers repeatedly flag a person's authentic verbal tics as AI filler, and acting on every flag removes exactly the habits that make the text sound like the user.

### Voice profile template

Copy into `convos/{user}/voice_profile.md` on first use (see above):

```
# Voice profile: {user}

Tier 1 — authorship guaranteed (the user's C: comments and bare requests in LOGs):
- {path} — {register, e.g. "long argumentative comments"} (grep "{distinctive phrase}")

Tier 2 — confirmed AI-free by the user:
- {path or link} — {register}

Excluded — mixed or suspect authorship, do NOT use as voice samples:
- {path} — {why, e.g. "user: parts had LLM help"}

Voice sketch (a hint, not a substitute for reading the samples):
- {habit} — e.g. "{short verbatim example}"
```

## Procedure

If the user has no voice profile yet, run "First use" above → draft (in the user's voice if a profile exists) → sweep against the checklist and fix clusters → add missing human markers → for public or high-stakes text (posts, applications, presentations; routine one-to-one emails may skip this), have a fresh sub-agent read it with "does any part of this read as AI-written? point at specific lines", without telling it who drafted the text → after any revision round, re-read start-to-finish for revision artifacts (checklist item 5) → final read-aloud pass → **citation gate before publishing**: a fresh sub-agent reads ONLY the final text, then looks up every cited source and checks that it exists and supports the exact claim the text attaches to it. This simulates a reader with no background and catches interpretation overreach, not just fake links; a citation being real is not the same as it saying what your sentence needs → for venues that screen with a detector (see Known screening), the detector check.
